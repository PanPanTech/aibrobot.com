A companion figure holds a pose because something inside it, and something under it, are doing the work. The photograph makes that look effortless. The first time you let go of a 40 kg body, you find out whether the stand was a real one.

## Posing

Move one joint at a time. Support the torso with your forearm, not with a pinch of skin. Wrists, ankles, and the neck are the places people break by hurrying. If a joint stops, stop. Forcing it past a stop is how a finger splits or a bolt loosens. Seat the figure before you attempt a stand, and put a towel down so the surface is not learning the shape of your floor the hard way.

The expression-head figure is posed the same way in the body. The head and neck have the limited motion described in its notes. You do not “walk” it into a corner. You carry it, or you do not move it.

## Stands

A stand has to carry the weight at the height, not just look stable in an empty room. This catalog includes bodies at 45 kg and 51 kg. A light photography stand is a tip waiting for a moment. Check the rating, the base width, and whether the clamp grabs a frame without cutting the material. A stand that works for a 157 cm body may be wrong for a 175 cm body even at a similar weight, because the leverage is higher.

Feet that are listed as standing feet on the silicone options sheet are a different question from a metal stand. If you need standing feet, ask for them as an option and wait for confirmation. Do not assume every body was built to balance unaided. Most of these figures are meant to be supported.

## Storage

Cool, dry, and out of the sun. A breathable cover beats a sealed bag, which traps moisture and odor. Do not leave the figure folded at the waist for a long storage. Flat spots happen where the weight rests. Shift it now and then, or store it on its back with the head supported so the neck is not the hinge for the whole mass. Powder an elastomer before a long pause if the surface is tacky, and keep the powder out of any electronic port.

If you share a home, the cover and a closed door are the privacy plan. They are also the plan that keeps a visitor from deciding to “just sit” the figure up by one arm.

Clothing left on for months can dye the surface and can trap damp. Store the figure clean, in light cloth or in nothing, and keep the outfits elsewhere. Wigs can stay if they are dry and not pulling on implanted hair. If the hair is implanted, a loose cover is kinder than a tight hood.

When you bring the figure out, give it the same first-week look: joints, surface, and, if it has a voice box or an expression head, a dry check that the port is intact. Storage is successful when the figure you unpack is the figure you packed. That is a modest goal, and it is the right one.
